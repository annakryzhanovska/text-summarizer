Beekeeping has become a common hobby in many large cities over the last twenty years.
Rooftops, balconies and community gardens now host thousands of hives in places like Berlin, London and New York.
City bees often produce more honey than bees in the countryside.
The reason is that cities offer a long flowering season, with parks, street trees and private gardens blooming one after another.
Farmland, by contrast, is frequently planted with a single crop that flowers for only a few weeks.
Pesticide use is also lower in many urban areas than on intensively farmed land.
However, more hives do not automatically mean more pollination for the city.
Honey bees compete with wild bees for the same flowers, and wild bees are often the more important pollinators for native plants.
Some studies suggest that a dense population of hives can push wild bees out of small green spaces.
For this reason several cities now advise new beekeepers to plant flowers before they set up a hive.
A balcony full of herbs and wildflowers can feed both honey bees and wild bees.
Beekeepers also need to learn how to manage swarms, because a swarm in a busy street can frighten people.
Most local beekeeping associations offer courses that cover hive care, swarm control and disease prevention.
With good planning, urban beekeeping can support both honey production and the wider insect population.
